# Combat choreography: space, tactics, causality, consequence

## Core principle

A convincing fight is not a catalogue of moves. It is a sequence of changing tactical problems. Every significant action should affect position, initiative, information, balance, options, or risk.

**Write what the action changes, not just what the body does.**

## 1. Establish the tactical frame

Before drafting a combat scene, determine:
- **Objective:** kill, escape, delay, capture, protect, disarm, break through, hold ground, or survive;
- **Participants:** skill, strength, reach, armor, weapons, injuries, fatigue, discipline, and willingness to kill;
- **Space:** distance, footing, elevation, cover, obstacles, exits, visibility, crowding, and nearby allies;
- **Constraints:** time, noise, rules, fear, orders, civilians, limited ammunition, light, weather, or terrain;
- **Stakes:** what happens if a fighter wins, loses, retreats, or delays.

Do not dump all of this as exposition. Establish only what the reader needs, then use it consistently.

## 2. Track position and orientation

Keep an internal spatial ledger:
- where each participant is;
- which direction they face;
- approximate distance and reach;
- which hand holds what;
- whether a weapon is free, trapped, dropped, damaged, or out of line;
- who is balanced, stumbling, pinned, kneeling, prone, or obstructed;
- what lies between the fighters.

Avoid impossible position jumps. A fighter cannot cross several metres, change sides, recover a dropped weapon, and attack again in one instant unless the prose accounts for the time and movement.

Re-anchor the reader after a major displacement, a change in participants, a fall, a retreat, or a transition between phases. Do not repeat every footstep.

## 3. Use tactical beats

A useful beat often follows:

**intention → action → response or resistance → changed situation → next choice**

Example structure (not a mandatory formula):
1. One fighter probes for an opening.
2. The opponent denies it or counteracts.
3. The exchange changes distance, balance, guard, or position.
4. Someone recognizes an opportunity or makes a mistake.
5. The next action exploits, fails to exploit, or misreads that change.

Not every beat needs all five parts, but important outcomes must be causally legible. A strike should not land merely because the narrator says it does.

## 4. Make skill visible through decisions

Competence is not a stream of successful attacks. Show it through:
- controlling distance and timing;
- protecting vulnerable lines;
- forcing the opponent to react;
- using terrain, feints, angles, teamwork, or patience;
- recognizing a bad exchange and disengaging;
- exploiting a specific mistake;
- conserving energy and choosing when not to attack.

Skilled fighters can still be surprised, injured, frightened, or wrong. Their competence should improve their decisions, not make them invulnerable.

## 5. Respect weapons, armor, and bodies

Use weapon properties consistently: reach, balance, edge, point, weight, handling, and limitations. Account for armor and clothing without making either magical. A blow to a protected area may bruise, stagger, exhaust, or impair movement without causing a clean wound; an exposed gap may be dangerous but difficult to target.

Physical actions have time and costs. Consider balance, leverage, grip, pain, breath, fatigue, blood loss, shock, and restricted movement. A severe injury should affect subsequent choices and capabilities unless a clear reason explains otherwise.

Avoid technical claims that exceed the scene's established level of realism. If the world uses stylized or supernatural combat, obey its rules consistently.

## 6. Make outcomes earned

A decisive hit, disarm, escape, or reversal should follow from identifiable causes such as:
- superior positioning or timing;
- numerical advantage or teamwork;
- terrain or visibility;
- fatigue, injury, equipment failure, or panic;
- a tactical error, feint, distraction, or false assumption;
- luck, when luck is genuinely part of the story.

Do not let the protagonist win simply because they are the protagonist. Do not let a less capable fighter defeat a superior opponent without a credible mechanism. If the outcome is intentionally improbable, make the cost or luck visible.

## 7. Group fights and battlefield scenes

For multiple combatants, keep track of local geometry and command:
- who can actually reach whom;
- which fighters are occupied or screened by others;
- how formations, bottlenecks, terrain, morale, and visibility shape action;
- how orders travel and whether they arrive in time;
- what the viewpoint character can perceive amid noise, fear, smoke, and movement.

Do not describe a large battle as if one person can observe the entire field with perfect clarity. Alternate local scenes with summary, reports, or broader narration when needed.

For commanders, distinguish tactical intent from execution. Orders can be delayed, misunderstood, disobeyed, or rendered irrelevant by changing conditions.

## 8. Pacing and prose

Slow down at reversals, near misses, consequential decisions, injuries, disarms, and shifts of initiative. Compress repetitive exchanges that do not change the situation.

Use a few precise sensory details—sound, impact, breath, footing, pain, visibility—rather than a generic flood of gore. Make action readable before making it ornate.

Vary the rhythm:
- short sentences can convey sudden danger;
- longer sentences can track a complex movement or accumulating pressure;
- pauses can show assessment, fear, exhaustion, or a change of plan.

Avoid repetitive patterns such as “he struck; she dodged; he struck again” without tactical development.

## 9. Combat continuity audit

After drafting, check:
- [ ] Every participant's location and weapon state remain plausible.
- [ ] Movement, distance, and timing make sense.
- [ ] The cause of each major injury, disarm, retreat, or victory is clear.
- [ ] Skill and physical capability match established characterization.
- [ ] Fatigue, wounds, armor, terrain, and numbers matter where relevant.
- [ ] The viewpoint does not know more than it could perceive.
- [ ] The outcome matches the draft unless the author authorized a change.
- [ ] The scene is not padded with repeated exchanges that do not change the tactical situation.

## 10. Protect canon in combat

You may invent connective movements and minor exchanges to dramatize an established fight. Do not independently decide a major battle's winner, kill a recurring character, reveal a secret technique, establish a new supernatural power, or change the political result unless the draft or author authorizes it.

If the draft gives a result but little choreography, invent a plausible path to that result without changing its strategic meaning. If several materially different paths are possible and each would change characterization or later events, choose the least committing plausible path or flag the choice.
