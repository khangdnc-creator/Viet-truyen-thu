# Dialogue rewriting: preserve intent, rebuild the exchange

## Core rule

**A draft line is usually a statement of what the character needs to communicate, not a sentence that must survive unchanged.** Unless the author explicitly marks dialogue as exact, rewrite it substantially when expanding or polishing a scene.

Do not preserve a line merely because it is already grammatical. If the draft is rough, functional, repetitive, or written to convey plot information, reconstruct the exchange in a way that sounds like people with specific motives speaking under specific circumstances.

## 1. Extract the communicative payload

Before writing a line, identify:
- **Information:** what must be communicated or concealed?
- **Intent:** what is the speaker trying to make the other person do, believe, feel, or reveal?
- **Power:** who has leverage, and what does each person risk?
- **Emotion:** what feeling drives the line, including feelings the speaker hides?
- **Subtext:** what is implied but not said?
- **Outcome:** what changes because the line is spoken?

Keep the payload and rebuild the wording. A threat can remain a threat without keeping the same sentence. A confession can preserve the same fact while changing its rhythm, hesitation, and emotional exposure.

## 2. Rewrite at the level of structure, not synonyms

Weak adaptation:
- Draft: “You betrayed me.”
- Rewrite: “You have betrayed me.”

This changes almost nothing.

Substantive adaptation might instead use accusation, evidence, evasion, or a controlled question depending on the scene:
- “When did you decide I was worth selling?”
- “You knew what that order would cost us.”
- “Tell me there was another way. Look at me and tell me.”

These are examples of different dramatic strategies, not interchangeable replacements. Choose only a line that fits the actual character, context, and intended meaning.

## 3. Dialogue must belong to the speaker

Differentiate voices through:
- education, profession, region, class, age, and cultural context;
- temperament, confidence, fear, patience, vanity, bluntness, or caution;
- relationship with the listener;
- whether the speaker is speaking publicly, privately, under threat, or while hiding something;
- preferred tactics: command, bargain, joke, question, silence, technical precision, emotional appeal, or evasion.

Do not reduce voice to catchphrases or accents. A character's voice is the pattern of what they notice, how they reason, what they avoid, and how they exert pressure.

## 4. Dialogue should be interactive

Avoid writing a sequence of speeches where each person simply delivers their prepared point. Let the exchange respond to itself:
- one character misunderstands or challenges a premise;
- another changes tactics after resistance;
- a direct question receives an evasive answer;
- a concession carries a price;
- a silence or gesture alters the balance;
- an interruption occurs only when it expresses urgency, power, or emotion.

Not every exchange needs conflict. Intimacy, humor, ritual, grief, and cooperation can also create movement.

## 5. Preserve information; improve delivery

Keep essential facts and decisions, but change how they are conveyed when appropriate:
- exposition can become a dispute, a correction, a warning, a question, or an incomplete explanation;
- a blunt declaration can become subtext if the scene benefits from restraint;
- repeated information can be cut if the reader already knows it;
- a character can reveal only part of what they know if concealment is plausible.

Never obscure information so much that the reader cannot understand the scene's necessary facts. Subtext should add a second layer, not replace basic clarity.

## 6. Exact-text exceptions

Preserve wording exactly only when:
- the author explicitly says to keep it verbatim;
- it is a canonical quotation, oath, legal clause, prophecy, poem, slogan, catchphrase, or repeated line whose exact wording matters;
- the task is proofreading or copyediting with a clear instruction not to rewrite;
- the exact wording is a deliberate clue or reveal.

If uncertain whether a line is intentionally fixed, normally adapt it while preserving its meaning. If the line may be a crucial clue or established quotation, retain it and flag the uncertainty only if needed.

## 7. Avoid artificial “better dialogue”

Do not make every line witty, poetic, threatening, or quotable. Do not add clever comebacks simply to display writing skill. Realistic dialogue includes practical questions, awkward phrasing, incomplete answers, repetition under stress, and silence.

Avoid:
- every character speaking in the same polished register;
- characters explaining facts they both know only for the reader;
- overuse of names, rhetorical questions, and dramatic pauses;
- emotion labels repeated after the dialogue already conveys the emotion;
- excessive action beats attached to every sentence;
- speeches that summarize a character's entire psychology;
- modern slang or elaborate archaism inconsistent with the setting.

## 8. Dialogue revision test

After drafting, compare the output to the original dialogue:
1. Are essential facts and decisions preserved?
2. Has the wording been meaningfully rebuilt rather than lightly paraphrased?
3. Does each speaker have a distinct reason to say this now?
4. Does the reply respond to the previous line?
5. Does the exchange change knowledge, leverage, emotion, or decision?
6. Have any new facts or commitments been introduced without authorization?
7. Are any original lines intentionally exact and therefore retained?

If most dialogue is still word-for-word identical to a rough draft, revise it again unless exact preservation was requested.
