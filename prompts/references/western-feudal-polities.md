# Western feudal polities and lordly societies

## Do not treat “the West” as one system
Choose the closest model and period: early medieval personal lordship, high-medieval feudal relations, late-medieval dynastic monarchy, a centralized early-modern state, a fantasy hybrid, or a wholly invented order. Titles and institutions changed across regions and centuries. Do not combine them casually as if they always coexisted.

## Distinguish types of power
A king's authority may depend on several overlapping sources:
- royal domain and household;
- hereditary nobles and their estates;
- vassalage, homage, oaths, fiefs, and customary obligations where appropriate;
- courts, sheriffs, bailiffs, stewards, royal officials, and tax collection;
- church or religious institutions;
- towns, guilds, charters, merchants, and local elites;
- mercenaries, household knights, levies, militias, and permanent troops.

Do not assume every noble owns a sovereign mini-state, every vassal is obedient, every knight is a landed noble, or every army consists of feudal levies. Define the actual arrangement in this world.

## Land, revenue, and rights
A lord's power is not simply “owns land.” Consider rents, crops, tolls, mines, forests, mills, ports, judicial rights, market privileges, inheritance, wardship, marriage, debts, and the ability to enforce claims. A title without revenue or armed support may be weak; a wealthy town or royal officer may be influential without being a great landowner.

## Crown versus nobility
The crown and noble houses are neither always enemies nor a single unified elite. Their relationship depends on mutual need. The monarch may need nobles for soldiers and local legitimacy; nobles may need royal courts, charters, protection, titles, or access to trade. A crisis can align some nobles with the crown and others against it. Each faction should have specific interests.

## Household and succession
A noble house includes more than the lord and heir: spouse, younger children, widowed relatives, guardians, stewards, chaplains, household knights, retainers, fosterlings, clients, and marriage allies may matter depending on scale. Do not introduce all of them automatically; include those who affect the scene.

Inheritance can be shaped by primogeniture, partition, election, custom, royal grant, conquest, marriage, or contested legitimacy. Do not assume one universal law. A widow, daughter, younger son, bastard, cousin, or regent has power only through the specific rights, allies, resources, and recognition available to them.

## Military obligations and war
Distinguish personal household troops, sworn retainers, vassal contingents, town militia, levies, mercenaries, professional soldiers, and allied forces. Their equipment, training, loyalty, readiness, and duration of service differ. A lord's order may be delayed or resisted; obligations can be disputed, negotiated, or bought out.

## Church, law, and custom
Religious authority can crown rulers, validate marriages, hold land, educate elites, shelter fugitives, arbitrate disputes, or compete with secular rulers. It is not automatically unified or morally consistent. Local custom and legal pluralism can matter: a royal court, manor, town charter, church court, and clan custom may treat the same dispute differently.

## Castles and governance
A castle is a military, administrative, household, and symbolic center. Its staff may include a castellan, steward, reeve, clerk, guards, servants, craftsmen, and supply workers. A lord does not personally perform every administrative task. Show the systems that make rule possible: accounts, stores, messengers, witnesses, seals, courts, and delegated authority.

## Women and political agency
Do not add women as token warriors or decorative court figures. Do not erase them by default either. Their possible roles depend on inheritance, marriage, widowhood, regency, property, convent/church office, trade, household administration, diplomacy, and the particular setting. A noblewoman may be politically central in one scene and constrained in another; show the mechanism and limits of her power.

## Dialogue and social distinction
A lord, landed knight, priest, merchant, reeve, peasant, mercenary, and court clerk should not all speak in the same register. Avoid universal “thee/thou” archaism, constant honorifics, and modern political vocabulary unless the world justifies them. Distinguish speech through priorities, education, region, profession, confidence, and relationship—not only accent.
