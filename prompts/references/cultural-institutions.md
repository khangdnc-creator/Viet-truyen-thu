# Cultural and institutional coherence

## Purpose
Use this file to model societies as functioning systems rather than collections of costumes, names, and stereotypes. The goal is not to force realism onto every fantasy world; it is to make each world's rules internally legible and consequential.

## Start with questions, not labels
Before writing a politically or culturally important scene, establish only the details that matter:
1. Who has legitimate authority, and why do people accept it?
2. Who actually controls armed force, revenue, land, food, law, appointments, and information?
3. How are offices and property acquired, inherited, granted, sold, or lost?
4. Which institutions compete, cooperate, or check one another?
5. How do class, gender, age, kinship, religion, occupation, region, and wealth affect a person's choices?
6. What does an ordinary person owe the state, lord, clan, temple, sect, or household?
7. What does this society consider shameful, lawful, sacred, disloyal, or merely imprudent?
8. How do war, famine, succession crises, migration, trade, and technological change alter the system?

Do not answer every question in exposition. Use the answers to make choices and consequences credible.

## Institutions before cultural shorthand
A culture should be visible through practices: who sits where, who speaks first, how a petition is submitted, who pays for a funeral, who can carry weapons in a palace, who can own land, how disputes are settled, how a marriage affects inheritance, who feeds an army, and how an order travels.

Avoid treating clothing, food, architecture, honorifics, or ritual as a substitute for institutional understanding. Decorative details are useful only when they support the scene or reveal status, belief, resources, or history.

## No one-trait houses, nations, sects, or peoples
A group may have a public reputation, comparative advantage, founding myth, or institutional specialty, but none of these should determine every member's personality or every policy.
- A cavalry house can have competing branches: one favors raids, one prefers estate management, another serves at court, and another resents the family's reputation.
- A scholarly sect can contain pragmatists, mystics, administrators, rival schools, and ambitious patrons.
- A military state still needs agriculture, merchants, judges, diplomats, religious institutions, and internal critics.
- A merchant republic can have factions divided by port, commodity, debt, guild, family marriage, or foreign patronage.
- A clan may share ancestry without sharing wealth, loyalty, doctrine, or political goals.

For each major group, define a small number of **structural pressures**, not a single personality: resources it depends on, offices it controls, threats it fears, rivals it needs, internal divisions, and public reputation. Then let individuals respond differently to those pressures.

## Reputation is not truth
Distinguish:
- how the group describes itself;
- how rivals describe it;
- what outsiders believe;
- what its members actually do;
- what the institution rewards or punishes.

A house called honorable may be scrupulous in public contracts but ruthless in private succession disputes. This is not random hypocrisy if the institution rewards both behaviors under different conditions.

## Cultural contact
Do not make cultural difference a simple contrast such as “East is ceremonial, West is blunt.” Identify the specific difference that matters: legal authority, lineage, land tenure, official appointment, religious legitimacy, hospitality, military command, diplomatic protocol, or concepts of status. Characters should misunderstand each other for plausible reasons and may learn over time.

## Show social order through consequences
If a character violates a norm, show who notices, what sanction is possible, who benefits, and whether the rule is actually enforceable. A custom with no social consequence is only set dressing.

## Research discipline
For real historical settings, use the exact period and region where possible. If reliable research is unavailable, avoid confident false specificity; write a restrained, plausible version and flag consequential uncertainty. For invented settings, record the author's rules as canon and do not “correct” them to match history.
