# Genre adaptation

Use only the relevant frameworks. Genre conventions never override canon. “Inspired by” is not a license to combine institutions from unrelated periods without a reason.

## Historical fiction and alternate history
Distinguish documented history, fiction, and point of divergence. Track food, transport, communication, medicine, law, hierarchy, military organization, taxation, and technology. Avoid unearned modern knowledge. Trace consequences of divergences. Do not claim facts were verified unless research or supplied sources support that claim.

## Ancient Chinese and Chinese-inspired fantasy
Consult `eastern-court-and-xianxia.md`. First decide whether the setting resembles an actual dynasty, a loose historical composite, a fully invented imperial civilization, or a cultivation world with its own institutions. Do not blend Han, Tang, Song, Ming, and Qing practices indiscriminately. Titles, clothing, examinations, military offices, marriage customs, palace organization, and legal norms varied by period and cannot be treated as timeless “Chinese culture.”

## Xianxia and cultivation worlds
Cultivation changes the balance of power but does not erase the need for institutions. Define how sects, clans, imperial states, local governments, markets, spiritual resources, inheritance, discipleship, oaths, and law interact. A cultivator's power may challenge the state, but the story must explain why powerful cultivators obey, bargain with, ignore, or overthrow institutions. Treat realms, lifespans, resources, breakthroughs, and costs consistently.

## Western-inspired feudal fantasy
Consult `western-feudal-polities.md`. Specify the period and political arrangement: royal domain, vassalage, hereditary lordship, church authority, towns, charters, common law/custom, household retinues, paid troops, and central administration may coexist in different proportions. Do not assume every noble personally commands every soldier or that every kingdom is a uniform pyramid of feudal obligations.

## Epic and political fantasy
Treat succession, land, taxation, marriage, religion, legitimacy, patronage, coercion, information, and military capacity as connected systems. Magic needs consistent limits, costs, access, and consequences. Do not make every political actor omniscient.

## Science fiction
Respect technology and speculative rules. Account for time, distance, energy, communication, reliability, and institutional consequences. Do not introduce effortless solutions without limits.

## Mystery and crime
Distinguish clue, inference, suspicion, testimony, and proof. Track who knows each clue and when. Preserve intended reveal timing. Ensure the resolution follows from evidence rather than a culprit or ability introduced at the last moment.

## Horror
Build dread through uncertainty, vulnerability, atmosphere, and consequence. Keep threat rules consistent. Preserve deliberate ambiguity without creating accidental confusion.

## Romance and relationship-driven fiction
Track knowledge, boundaries, desire, trust, power imbalance, and changes in intimacy. Make relationship turns arise from choices and consequences. Preserve the intended emotional promise.

## Literary fiction
Allow ambiguity, mood, interiority, and unresolved tension when purposeful. Style should serve perception, theme, and character rather than become ornament.

## Adventure, war, and political thriller
Clarify objectives, constraints, stakes, and consequences. Keep geography, timing, supply, command, intelligence, and communication plausible. Luck may matter but should not replace causality.

## Cross-genre
Identify the dominant reader promise for the chapter and combine conventions without violating the author's rules. When cultures meet, preserve asymmetry in knowledge and assumptions; neither side should automatically understand the other's etiquette, institutions, or strategic logic.
