# Gender and cast demographics without tokenism

## Rule
Do not add characters merely to satisfy a perceived diversity quota, and do not exclude characters merely to satisfy a blanket demographic rule. Build the cast from the author's draft, the setting's institutions, the specific occupation and location, the period or world rules, and the needs of the scene.

## Scene-level questions
1. What group is this scene actually depicting: a royal guard, a council, a siege crew, a market, a sect assembly, a royal household, a village, or an entire army?
2. Who is eligible or likely to be present in this society and institution?
3. Who has a concrete reason to be here: duty, rank, kinship, expertise, command, labor, witness, negotiation, or chance?
4. Does the draft or canon specify the participants' gender or identity?
5. If a character is added, does the character alter causality, perspective, social texture, or a relevant relationship—or are they merely filling a slot?

## Historical realism is contextual, not a universal percentage
Many historical military institutions and frontline formations were male-dominated, sometimes overwhelmingly so. That does not justify declaring every historical or fantasy group 100% male by default. Demographics varied by period, region, class, occupation, emergency, and institutional rules. Court households, markets, religious communities, logistics, medical care, labor, espionage, and dynastic politics have different participant pools from a frontline unit.

For fantasy, follow the world's established rules. If women can hold military office, cultivate, inherit, or rule, establish how common or exceptional this is and what institutions permit or resist it. Do not silently import modern norms; do not silently import a universal historical exclusion either.

## No token characters
A character should not exist only to be “the woman,” “the minority,” “the outsider,” or the representative of a demographic group. Give them an individual objective, skills, limits, loyalties, social position, and consequences. Do not make every female character unusually exceptional to justify her presence; ordinary women may appear in ordinary roles where the setting makes that plausible.

## Respect fixed cast details
Never change the gender, identity, kinship, rank, or established role of a supplied character without authorization. Do not add a new character to a group if the author has explicitly defined the membership or requested no additions. If the draft lists a named all-male royal guard, keep it all-male unless the author asks for a change. If it lists a mixed council, do not “correct” it to a historical stereotype.

## Reporting
Do not announce that a cast has been “balanced.” If demographic composition matters to the story, show the institution or social norm that produces it and the consequences it has. Otherwise, simply write the scene.
