# Eastern court, ancient China, and xianxia

## First decide which kind of setting this is
Do not use “ancient China” as a single timeless setting. Choose one:
- **Specific historical period:** follow its actual institutions and terminology as closely as the project requires.
- **Historical composite:** borrow selectively, but keep the combination coherent and avoid presenting it as exact history.
- **Invented imperial civilization:** create institutions that make sense together; historical analogies are inspiration, not constraints.
- **Xianxia / cultivation world:** decide which imperial, clan, sect, local, commercial, and supernatural institutions exist, and how they share or contest power.

When the period is unspecified and the choice materially affects titles, succession, examinations, clothing, law, or palace structure, ask one concise question or use neutral terminology until clarified.

## The imperial state is more than the emperor
For a centralized imperial model, consider the interaction among:
- emperor and imperial household;
- empress, consorts, princes, princesses, and maternal kin;
- central ministries, censorial or supervisory bodies, secretariats/councils where period-appropriate;
- civil officials and military command;
- provincial, prefectural, county, or analogous local administration;
- tax, grain, transport, labor, military recruitment, and legal systems;
- local gentry, landed families, merchants, temples, schools, and popular networks.

Do not automatically include every office. Select the institutions that belong to the chosen period or invented system. The emperor may possess supreme formal authority while still being constrained by information, succession politics, fiscal limits, military commanders, court coalitions, custom, or the practical capacity of local government.

## Palace and succession politics
Model the palace as an institution with rules and competing interests, not just a room full of schemers. Ask:
- Who can enter which spaces and approach the ruler?
- Who controls documents, seals, schedules, guards, and access?
- How are heirs recognized, educated, ranked, and publicly presented?
- How do the empress's natal family, consort families, maternal relatives, eunuchs or palace servants (if appropriate to the period), princes, ministers, and military commanders gain influence?
- What legal, ritual, or political basis supports a succession claim?
- What are the costs of openly opposing the throne?

Do not assume every consort is a mastermind, every minister is corrupt, every eunuch is a villain, or every prince is a rival. Their power depends on actual office, access, patronage, faction, competence, and circumstance.

## Clan, family, and officialdom
A lineage can provide education, marriage networks, reputation, property, and mutual aid; it does not guarantee that every member shares a single goal. Distinguish lineage prestige from formal office. A powerful clan may influence appointments without literally owning the state. An official's post may derive from appointment rather than hereditary ownership of the territory.

Do not collapse clan, household, lineage, sect, school, and political faction into synonyms. Each should have a distinct membership rule and source of authority.

## Exams, learning, and bureaucracy
Use examination systems only when they fit the chosen period or fictional design. Distinguish examination success, recommendation, hereditary privilege, purchase of office, military appointment, court patronage, and practical competence. A bureaucratic state is never staffed by “merit” alone, nor should all officials be reduced to one corrupt bloc.

## Xianxia: cultivation does not replace society
Define the interaction among:
- **Sect:** membership, master-disciple lineage, resources, discipline, succession, external obligations, and internal factions.
- **Clan:** bloodline, marriage, inherited techniques, property, descendants, and family branches.
- **Imperial/local state:** taxation, law, land, ordinary people, roads, markets, conscription, and relations with cultivators.
- **Independent cultivators:** livelihood, reputation, alliances, access to resources, and legal status.
- **Markets and resource networks:** spirit stones, pills, herbs, mines, caves, manuals, transport, information, and monopolies.
- **Power tiers:** what each level can reliably do, what it cannot do, how rare it is, and what it costs.

If a cultivator can destroy a city alone, explain why the empire survives: deterrence, rival cultivators, oaths, scarce resources, geography, institutions, spiritual constraints, or simply because the premise says it does. Do not let a mortal bureaucracy control beings vastly beyond its reach without a credible mechanism.

A sect is not automatically a school, clan, monastery, corporation, or army. Define which functions it actually performs. A righteous sect may have political interests; a demonic sect may contain rules, loyalties, and competing philosophies. Labels should not replace behavior.

## Social hierarchy and everyday life
Track how rank affects address, seating, clothing, marriage, inheritance, education, legal penalties, movement, and access to resources. Avoid making every commoner ignorant or every aristocrat refined. People across classes have practical knowledge, ambitions, loyalties, and local identities.

## Gender and family in imperial settings
Do not impose modern egalitarian norms by default, but do not assume every woman is powerless or every elite woman is a political mastermind. Determine property rights, marriage rules, inheritance, education, ritual roles, palace access, labor, and local variation from the setting. A woman can exercise influence through a recognized office, kinship, wealth, ritual authority, intelligence, or personal relationships—but the mechanism must exist in the world.

## Dialogue, etiquette, and worldview
Use rank-appropriate forms of address and indirectness where context supports them. Etiquette is not universal: a battlefield order, private family dispute, court audience, sect initiation, and marketplace negotiation should not all sound alike. Characters may be blunt, funny, vulgar, affectionate, or irreverent within their own social context.
